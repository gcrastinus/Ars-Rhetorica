Ars Rhetorica

The third art of the trivium
Rhetoric is the faculty of observing, in any given case, the available means of persuasion of what is true. Grammar asks whether the utterance is well formed; logic asks whether what is said is true. Rhetoric asks whether the hearer is moved to see the truth that has been expressed. This course is Aristotelian in its principles, Ciceronian in its arrangement, and Augustinian in its offices, because for Augustine the orator aims to teach, delight, and move (docere, delectare, flectere).
How to Proceed

Where to begin. Open the first tutorial, The Art of Rhetoric, and read it through. Then try The three species at difficulty I. Most sets are ten questions. At difficulty I each is worth ten points, and a clean run of ten is 100. The six offices, the paired debates, and Gorgias’s Helen are sets of six; the Christian orator is a set of eight. A counter shows how far along you are.

What to do in an exercise. Read the excerpt, then choose your answer. A right answer gains points; a wrong answer loses some. Keys 1–4 choose a multiple-choice option; Enter submits. Where the original Latin or Greek can be displayed, the Show the Latin button shows it. Sources for all texts are given in place.

The special sections. Gorgias’s Helen is a tutorial and a figures set of its own (persuasion as a drug; the four aitiai). Antiphon’s Second Tetralogy is kept as forensic. Some key debates from Thucydides (Archidamus and Sthenelaidas, Cleon and Diodotus, Pericles, Catiline and Caesar) are paired below as well. The texts also include Augustine on the Christian orator and some passages from the Confessions.

For a general introduction to the classical liberal arts and the trivium, see Introduction to the Trivium.

How the Guided Path Works

Two paths. Free Practice opens every exercise at once and keeps no record for the Guided Path. The Guided Path opens the exercises in order, judges when each is passed and when it is mastered, and brings mastered exercises back for spaced review. Its record is kept only in this browser, and only once tracking is turned on under Progress & Review; the Export button there saves a copy.

The order of the path. The exercises open in eight steps, in the order of the art: the three species; the three pisteis; the enthymeme, the issue, and the topics; character and the passions; the parts of the oration; style and the figures; the whole case (Antiphon, the paired debates, and the whole-case set); and last, Augustine’s Christian orator and Gregory’s contrasting audiences. Each step opens when every exercise of the step before it is passed. The study decks are always open, and the optional set of further figures is never required.

Passing an exercise. An exercise is passed when nine of the last ten answers at Level III or above are correct, on at least two different days. Levels IV and V of an exercise open only after it is passed.

Mastery. An exercise is mastered (★) when eight of the last ten answers at Level V are correct, on at least two different days.

Review. A mastered exercise returns for review after 1, 3, 7, 16, and then 35 days. Each review asks three questions: if all three are right, the next review comes after the longer interval, but if one is missed, the exercise returns to daily review. The level of the questions rises as the intervals lengthen, from Level III to Level V. Today’s Review gathers everything that is due, interleaves it, and then names the next exercise to learn. A missed review never closes an exercise that has been opened.

Content reviews. After each Division’s decks and exercises comes a short review of the doctrine itself, one for each deck. It asks for definitions and distinctions from memory, and on the Guided Path it is ready one day after the deck is finished and one of its exercises is passed. These reviews open nothing and close nothing.

Recitation Tablet

What is recitation?

One of the oldest and best-proven ways to study is to read a section of a book, or study a topic, in one sitting, then close your notes and write down everything you remember, in as much detail as you can. This is called "recitation," and this tablet lets you do it.

After you finish studying a part of the course, close it and open the Recitation Tablet. Choose the part you just finished from the menu, then write down, in order, everything you can remember about it. This is not easy. But the difficulty is what produces results, especially the moment when you feel you remember nothing more and then keep searching.

When you have finished, click "See Full Recitation." You will see a complete outline of the material with every point stated simply. The outline is a checklist: go through it carefully and mark exactly what you included correctly and what you missed. The course will remind you to recite the same material again after a few days, since each new recall fixes it more firmly.

Write a fuller recitation to see the full one.
