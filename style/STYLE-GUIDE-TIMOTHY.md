## Two registers

| | **Expository (default for all app text)** | **Elevated (rare)** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | articles, logic overview, nominalism, history of theology | *Creation and Cosmos*, *From Light into Light* |
| Sentences | long, connected, explanatory | strings of rhetorical questions; short emphatic lines ("And it is.") |
| Stance | measured; concedes, then argues | hortatory ("What we need are…", "our task is…") |
| Use in apps | **always** | at most a closing invitation; probably **none in Trivium** |

## Core traits of the default voice

1. **Full, connected sentences.** His expository sentences average about 25–30 words (*Logic* 29, *Nominalism* 26). He joins clauses with semicolons and *but / so / therefore*. Short sentences carry a definition or an answer ("Logic is the art of reasoning well."). He doesn't use them for punch. (The current Trivium pages average 18 words, and 1 sentence in 5 is 8 words or fewer.)
2. **Question, then answer.** He states the question outright and answers it: *"So, what are words? … Words, therefore, are signs of things. But what are signs?"* and *"The nominalist answer is this: …"*
3. **Explicit logical signposts.** He opens sentences with *So,* / *Now,* / *But*, and uses *therefore, however, thus, that is, for example, more precisely, in general, in so far as, properly speaking*, and enumerations (*First … Second …*). He shows the reasoning instead of leaving it implied.
4. **"We" for shared inquiry.** He writes with *we* and *our*, and uses *I* sparingly for his own judgments or plan ("Let me start by…", "I argue"). He almost never addresses *you*; it appears only in passing examples ("the elm tree behind your house"). He doesn't coach the reader.
5. **Names things exactly.** He names the subject outright (his edit put "the classical trivium (which is the coordinated studies of grammar, logic, and rhetoric)" in place of a vaguer phrase). He defines terms before using them and glosses Latin or Greek in parentheses ("(Latin: *res*)"). He states distinctions in pairs ("primarily an art … but also a science"), and refers to *St. Thomas / Aquinas* and *the classical tradition*.
6. **Measured concession instead of hype.** He grants what is true before arguing ("(which is true) … (which is also true)") and admits difficulty ("This is a counterintuitive claim in our time; but…"). He uses *seems* honestly. The tradition is the subject of the claim, not a "we must" ("that is where the tradition begins," not "where we must begin").
7. **Punctuation.** He uses many semicolons and parentheses (for glosses, asides, concessions), colons to introduce an answer or a list, and *that is* / *e.g.* / *i.e.*. He uses **very few dashes** (about 0.6 per 1,000 words, against 3.3 in the current app text).
8. **Plain, concrete examples, introduced as such.** Examples include slime molds, *cat* and *billee*, an association of lawyers, and a ball on a plane. He introduces them with *for example*, *to take the example of*, *compare X to Y*, or *consider*.
9. **What he cuts.** He cuts aphorisms and pithy closing lines ("they are where the reading is tried"), filler figures of speech ("a detour"), and vague institutional labels ("modern schooling" became "contemporary studies"). He also softens imperatives ("You should have a teacher" became "Ideally, you would have a teacher").

## Examples from his own writing

1. "Man is the rational animal. Rationality, reason, is his defining faculty. Logic is the art of reasoning well." (*Logic*)
2. "But this raises a question: What do we mean by “thing” (Latin: res)? Informally, a thing is anything that we can speak about…" (*Logic*)
3. "So, what are words? Informally, we most often use words to speak about things. Words, therefore, are signs of things. But what are signs?" (*Logic*)
4. "But concepts of the same thing are the same for everyone. This is a counterintuitive claim in our time; but it is also a foundational truth of the whole classical tradition and necessary for any account of logic." (*Logic*)
5. "Not all reasoning looks or sounds exactly like this, of course, but this is the simplest and defining structure that reasoning has." (*Logic*)
6. "To study slime molds, we would first have to know what kind of thing a slime mold is." (*What is nominalism?*)
7. "So, on the nominalist account, what kinds of things exist? The nominalist answer is this: there are no kinds of things." (*What is nominalism?*)
8. "Let me start by taking the key senses of the word “history” in English and how Aristotle and St. Thomas use the word." (*On Historical Theology*)
9. "(Lest we too readily suppose that all Greeks were philosophers, recall that many ordinary Greeks would still have thought in ways very similar to that of the Ancient Near East…)" (*Outline … of Logic in the Western Tradition*)
10. *Elevated register, for contrast:* "All truth is ultimately simple, like God. So, we should expect the truth about our place in the world and the order of creation to be simple too. And it is." (*From Light into Light*); "But the real questions are deeper." (*Creation and Cosmos*)

## What his edits show

- **6 Oct (welcome page):** He prefers precise naming over a general gesture ("understand the foundations of the classical trivium"). He uses an academic register ("contemporary studies", "mathematical representations"). The tradition carries the claim ("the tradition begins"). He cut the epigram ("they are where the reading is tried" became "do not replace study of the classic texts"), cut the figure "a detour", and made the scope explicit ("at the end *of this introduction*").
- **24 Sep (welcome page):** He turned a tacked-on "ideally" into a full statement ("That is the ideal.") and softened a "should".
- **4 Oct (six pages):** These are mainly doctrinal precision, and it isn't certain they were all in his own hand. Examples: "to a mind" became "to a knowing power"; "a concept" became "a concept, the formal sign proper"; and "taken as what is known" and "second intentions" were added. The lesson: when accuracy and smoothness conflict, he keeps the technical term and adds the qualification.

## AI-style tics to remove (from the *AI-style language cleanup* workflow, extended)

- **Coaching imperatives and second person** ("Look at…", "Start with…", "here is the sentence to hold on to"). Recast them as statements or as *we* / *let us* / *consider*.
- **Aphoristic closers and fragments** ("Three parts, and all three matter." / "Nobody is shut in anywhere." / "The sounds are local. What the sounds are for is not.").
- **Staccato runs of short sentences**, and **dramatic reveals** ("Something stands between… and it is doing work.").
- **Filler, hype, stock transitions:** *delve*, *it's important to note*, *crucially*, *not just X but Y*, reflexive lists of three.
- **Heavy dashes.** Use commas, parentheses, or a semicolon.
- **Casual idiom in place of the technical term** ("glued to a thing", "the causing runs", "lying about"). Use the proper term, with a short gloss if needed.
- **Keep exactly:** his terminology, Latin, citations, quotations, links, and meaning. Add no content.
- **Student-facing wording:** "sets of exercises," never "apps." (The Trivium welcome page still says "The three practice apps" and "live applications"; flag this for his decision.)
- **Report format:** OLD → NEW pairs unless he asks for the edits to be applied.

## Revision checklist

1. Read the whole page first.
2. Turn *you* into *we* (keep an occasional incidental *your* in an example).
3. Merge fragments and short runs into connected sentences, making the logical link explicit (*so, therefore, but, that is*).
4. State any question the paragraph answers, then answer it.
5. Cut the epigram at the end of each paragraph, or fold its point into the preceding sentence.
6. Replace dashes and idioms. Keep or restore the technical term.
7. Check that every claim, example, heading, citation, and quotation is unchanged in substance.
